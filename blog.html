Leading with Values: Lessons from Niren Chaudhary’s Aspire Mentorship Session

By Ndatimana Jean de Dieu · Reflection on Aspire Institute’s March 28, 2025 feature

Leadership is not only about having a title, achieving professional success or knowing how to manage a team. It is also about understanding what matters to us and making sure that our everyday actions reflect those values. An Aspire Institute feature about former Panera Brands CEO Niren Chaudhary’s mentorship session offers a valuable reminder that character and consistency are central to meaningful leadership.

Know the values that guide you

One of the most important lessons from Chaudhary’s experience is the need for clarity about personal values. Values such as courage, gratitude, integrity and resilience can help us make decisions, especially when circumstances are difficult. But naming our values is only the beginning. We must think carefully about what they mean and practise them through our choices and habits.

Let actions demonstrate your beliefs

A leader’s intentions may be sincere, but people experience leadership through what that person actually does. This means that our behavior should match the principles we say we believe in. Trust grows when people see consistency between our words, decisions and actions. It also requires humility: being willing to recognize mistakes, take responsibility and change course when necessary.

Keep learning, unlearning and relearning

Careers, communities and technology continue to change. Effective leaders remain curious and open to new information. They do not treat failure as proof that growth is impossible. Instead, they reflect on what did not work, learn from the experience and use those lessons to improve. Lifelong learning is therefore not simply about gaining more knowledge; it is also about being willing to question old assumptions.

Build character alongside competence

Skills and technical knowledge are important, but they are not enough on their own. Leadership also calls for empathy, courage, resilience, gratitude and a commitment to serve others. Chaudhary’s advice to young leaders emphasizes building character, continuing to learn and responding to challenges with resilience. These principles are relevant to students, entrepreneurs, professionals and anyone who wants to contribute positively to a community.

A lesson I want to carry forward

As someone developing through the Aspire Leaders Program and other learning experiences, I find this message especially meaningful. I want my growth in technology, entrepreneurship and community leadership to be guided not only by what I can achieve, but also by the values behind the work. For me, that means listening to people, staying open to feedback, acting responsibly and using what I learn to create opportunities for others.

Values-based leadership is built through repeated actions. It begins with knowing what matters, continues through daily habits and becomes visible in how we treat people and respond to challenges. The goal is not to appear perfect, but to keep becoming more thoughtful, accountable and useful to others.

Source and further reading: This is an original reflection based on Aspire Institute’s article, “Niren Chaudhary, Former Panera Brands CEO, Shares Powerful Insights on Values-Based Leadership”, published March 28, 2025.

Clean Water, Limited Resources: A Rwandan Student’s Journey Toward Affordable Water Solutions

By Ndatimana Jean de Dieu · Featured story

Growing up in Rwanda, I have seen how limited resources can shape the opportunities available to a community. Access to safe drinking water is one of the most important challenges because it affects health, education, household finances and dignity. My work on affordable water purification reflects a conviction that local challenges deserve practical solutions designed with local knowledge and resources.

Starting with a community problem

The project described in this story began with a simple but urgent question: how can communities improve the quality of their drinking water using materials and skills that are accessible locally? Working with a Science Club, I took part in examining water sources in Nkombo Island and the Rutsiro District. The initial investigation focused on water clarity, possible contamination and the practical barriers families face when seeking safer water.

Water quality is not one single measurement. It can involve microbial contamination, turbidity and chemical substances, each of which requires appropriate testing. The findings from our initial work encouraged us to explore a low-cost filtration approach rather than accept that expensive commercial equipment is the only option.

Developing an affordable filtration concept

My prototype brought together locally accessible filtration materials, including sand and activated charcoal, alongside a polymer membrane developed through school laboratory work. The goal was to investigate whether a simple, maintainable design could reduce visible impurities and selected contaminants while remaining affordable for rural households.

Project measurements reported during testing included reductions in turbidity and plastic microparticles, as well as changes in bacterial counts and nitrate readings. These early results helped guide further experimentation. They should be understood as project-reported findings, not as a substitute for independent laboratory validation or certification that the treated water is safe to drink. Effective water treatment must be verified against the relevant drinking-water standards, including microbial and chemical testing.

Affordability and local maintenance matter

A water solution must work technically, but it must also fit the economic realities of the people expected to use it. A system that is too expensive to purchase, maintain or repair will not be sustainable for many rural families. This is why the project considered locally available materials, operating costs and the possibility of community-level maintenance.

The prototype was estimated to cost about US$18 to build, with a projected operating cost of approximately US$0.025 per litre over two years. These are preliminary project estimates; actual costs will depend on production scale, testing, replacement materials, installation and maintenance. Comparing solutions fairly requires measuring the full cost over their useful life.

From a school project toward community use

The work moved beyond the laboratory into pilot discussions and use in communities in the Rutsiro District. The project account describes three communities and approximately 340 people reached by the initiative. Community participation is essential: residents need to understand how a system works, how to maintain it and when water must be tested or treated again.

For me, this experience reinforced that innovation is not simply inventing a device. It also means listening to users, learning from testing, improving the design and building trust through transparent evidence.

Learning through setbacks and persistence

My educational journey has included challenges as well as opportunities. I learned that a setback does not have to determine the direction of a person's future. Support from educators, the chance to continue learning and a willingness to work consistently helped me regain momentum and focus on what I could improve.

My interest in science and practical problem-solving grew through hands-on learning. Building and experimenting with simple devices, exploring chemistry and working with others taught me to connect classroom concepts with everyday needs. These experiences shaped my belief that young people can contribute meaningfully when they are encouraged to ask questions, experiment responsibly and keep learning.

A vision for sustainable water innovation in Rwanda

My long-term ambition is to contribute to a research and engineering centre in Rwanda focused on sustainable water solutions and environmental chemistry. Such a centre could support water-quality testing, develop affordable treatment approaches suited to different communities, train young people in science and engineering, and create opportunities for local innovation and employment.

That vision will require continued study, partnerships, rigorous research and independent evaluation. Any future system must meet applicable public-health standards and be safe, reliable and maintainable before it is scaled.

Why locally led solutions matter

Clean water is a global challenge, but the most useful solutions must respond to local conditions. Communities understand their own needs, constraints and resources. When students, researchers, local leaders and residents work together, they can develop approaches that are more relevant and more likely to last.

My journey has taught me that limited resources do not mean a lack of ideas or potential. With curiosity, practical education, careful testing and collaboration, young people can help turn community problems into opportunities for meaningful innovation.

About the author

Ndatimana Jean de Dieu is a Rwandan student and emerging technology and community innovator. His interests include science, computer science, practical engineering, entrepreneurship and youth leadership. His clean-water work reflects his commitment to exploring affordable, locally relevant solutions for rural communities.

The Future of Leadership in an AI-Driven Africa

By Ndatimana Jean de Dieu · 6 minute read

Artificial intelligence is changing how people learn, work, communicate and solve problems. For Africa, this moment is not only about adopting new tools. It is an opportunity to develop leaders who understand technology and use it responsibly to improve people’s lives.

Leadership begins with people

Technology can process information quickly, but leadership still requires listening, empathy, courage and sound judgment. A leader must understand the needs of a community before deciding which tool or solution is appropriate. AI should support that process, not replace human responsibility.

Turning local challenges into opportunities

African communities face diverse challenges in agriculture, education, healthcare access and small-business growth. AI-enabled tools may help farmers access useful information, entrepreneurs organize business records and learners find educational resources. The strongest ideas begin with real needs and are tested with the people they are intended to serve.

Skills for the next generation

Young people can prepare by building digital literacy, critical thinking, communication and problem-solving skills. They should learn how AI systems work, question unreliable outputs, protect personal information and understand that generated answers can contain mistakes.

Responsible innovation matters

AI can reproduce bias, expose private information or make errors that affect important decisions. Responsible leaders should consider fairness, transparency, safety, accessibility and accountability. They should keep people involved in decisions that affect them.

A future built through collaboration

Africa’s future will be shaped by students, educators, entrepreneurs, researchers, public institutions and communities working together. We need spaces where young people can experiment, share knowledge and build solutions suited to local contexts.

The future belongs not simply to those who use the newest technology, but to those who use it with purpose. By combining human-centered leadership with practical AI skills, young Africans can help create a more inclusive and innovative future.

Building Technology Around Real Problems

By Ndatimana Jean de Dieu · 4 minute read

It is easy to become excited by a new technology, programming language or app idea. But a useful product does not begin with technology. It begins with a problem that matters to someone.

Start by listening

Before building, understand the people who experience the problem. Ask how they currently handle it, what makes the process difficult and what they have already tried. Respectful conversations often reveal details missed when a team makes assumptions.

Build a small first version

A first version should test the most important assumption with the least complexity possible. A simple prototype or small pilot can reveal whether the idea is useful. Feedback should shape the next version.

Measure usefulness

Downloads, features and social-media attention do not automatically mean a product creates value. Ask whether it saves time, reduces costs, improves access or helps users make better decisions.

Design for the context

Solutions should reflect real conditions such as connectivity, language, affordability, device access and users’ existing habits. The goal is not to prove that a team can use impressive technology. The goal is to create something people genuinely find useful.

Learning as a Tool for Opportunity

By Ndatimana Jean de Dieu · 4 minute read

Learning is more than preparing for an examination or collecting certificates. It is a continuous process of developing the knowledge, habits and confidence needed to contribute meaningfully to society.

Stay curious and take ownership

Curiosity encourages us to ask questions and explore ideas beyond the classroom. Taking ownership means setting learning goals, finding reliable resources and making time to practise. Progress often comes from small, consistent steps.

Turn knowledge into practice

Projects, volunteering, teamwork and practical challenges connect theory with real situations. A student learning computer science can build a small application, document what was learned and ask others to test it.

Learn with other people

Mentors, peers and learning communities expose us to new perspectives. Sharing what we know is also a powerful way to deepen understanding.

Use opportunities with purpose

Programmes and networks can open doors, but their value depends on what we do with the experience. Apply what you learn to real challenges and share useful knowledge with others.

With curiosity, discipline and a willingness to serve, learning can help individuals grow while strengthening their communities.

About the author: Ndatimana Jean de Dieu is a Rwandan computer science student interested in artificial intelligence, entrepreneurship, agriculture innovation and youth leadership. These articles are personal reflections.
