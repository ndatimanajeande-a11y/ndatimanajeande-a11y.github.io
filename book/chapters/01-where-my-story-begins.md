# Chapter One: Where My Story Begins

My story begins in Rwanda, in Mataba Cell, Terimbere Village, Rutsiro District. I later grew up on Nkombo Island in Lake Kivu, a place that became an important part of my identity and shaped how I understand community, opportunity and the challenges people face when resources are limited.

Growing up, I became interested in science and the way the world works. Yet the classrooms around me did not always have the laboratories, equipment or practical learning opportunities that could bring scientific ideas to life. Much of what I learned came from textbooks, explanations and imagination.

One small book in the school library changed the way I thought about science. It described advanced laboratories in other parts of the world and showed me what scientific investigation could look like when students had access to proper equipment. I began to imagine myself working in such environments. I wanted not only to read about scientific discoveries, but to understand how they were made.

## Learning to understand, not memorise

During Senior Four at GS Saint Joseph in Nyamasheke, I encountered difficult topics in chemistry, including atomic structure and mass spectrometry. My mathematics performance fell to 55%, while my chemistry theory result was around 65%. These results challenged my confidence.

A classmate, Krispin, helped me approach organic chemistry questions differently. Rather than simply telling me which multiple-choice answer was correct, he explained why an answer worked and why the other options did not. His approach taught me to reason from principles instead of depending only on memorisation.

During the holidays, I continued working on advanced science problems with my primary-school friend Henriette. We encountered an International Chemistry Olympiad question involving laboratory apparatus, procedures and experimental data. Without access to the equipment, it was difficult to visualise what the question described. I used my phone to watch laboratory demonstrations and connect the written instructions with practical procedures.

When I returned to GS Saint Pierre Nkombo for Senior Six, I sought additional guidance from Prof. Eric during office hours. I wanted to understand the mechanisms behind chemical reactions and their practical relevance, not just reproduce definitions.

## Turning classroom knowledge into community practice

During our weekly Science Hour, I led classmates in exploring applications of esterification and saponification. We used vegetable oils, esters and formulas from library resources to experiment with making liquid soap and hand sanitiser for neighbouring families on Nkombo Island.

Our first attempts were not perfect. The hard soap we produced was weak after three days. Instead of giving up, we reviewed the cold-process method, temperature control and reactant ratios. We adjusted our approach and refined the process.

That experience helped me understand that science is not only about obtaining the right answer in an examination. It is also about observing results, identifying problems, testing explanations and improving a process. Even a small experiment can teach patience, responsibility and the value of careful measurement.

In the 2024 Rwanda national examinations, I earned an A in Chemistry. I also earned an A in Entrepreneurship. Those results were important milestones after the difficulties I had faced in earlier years.

## Discovering technology and engineering

My chemistry achievement helped open opportunities to participate in maker-space sessions and Techrise STEM training at the American Center in Kigali. There, I moved beyond theoretical learning and began working with technology directly.

I assembled an FM radio, built a model engine and worked with a team of five to develop a regulated traffic-light system. Each activity gave me a different way to connect scientific thinking with engineering and problem-solving.

I began to see that chemistry, physics, mathematics and technology were not isolated subjects. They could work together to address practical needs.

## The vision taking shape

My long-term ambition is to pursue higher education and combine scientific knowledge with practical engineering. I want to contribute to sustainable water purification and sanitation systems that can serve island and rural communities across Rwanda.

Growing up around Lake Kivu helped me recognise how important water and sanitation are to everyday life. I want to develop solutions that are not only technically sound, but also affordable, maintainable and appropriate for the communities that need them.

I also hope to represent Rwanda as a scientist and innovator, showing that ideas and solutions can emerge from communities where access to resources has not always been easy.

My journey is still developing. But the curiosity that began with a small library book continues to guide me. I want to keep asking questions, learning from failure, building practical solutions and using education to create opportunities beyond myself.
